The first crossword series, rebuilt from the papers that copied it

The New York World printed a crossword every Sunday from December 1913, and its own pages survive badly. The papers that reprinted its FUN section kept a second copy of each puzzle. Here is how we are putting the series back together from them, and what is still missing.

Every Puzzle Project · 6 November 2026

The first crossword, Arthur Wynne's "Word-Cross Puzzle", ran in the New York World's Sunday supplement, FUN, on 21 December 1913. It is the most reproduced crossword there is. The puzzles that came after it, a new one every Sunday, are another matter. We know of no scans of the FUN section online. The few early pages that researchers have reproduced come from sources they rarely name, and the likely holders are private collections and library microfilm. For most Sundays of the crossword's first four years, nobody today can say what puzzle the World printed.

But the World did not keep its puzzle to itself. Almost from the start, other papers carried the FUN section, crossword and all. The Minneapolis Journal printed the World's puzzle on 1 February 1914, six weeks after the first one. The Pittsburgh Press followed in June 1916 and the Tacoma Daily Ledger that August; by 1917 the Los Angeles Sunday Tribune, the Shreveport Times, the Fort Wayne Journal-Gazette, the New Orleans States, the Salt Lake Telegram and the Boston Globe were running it too. Each of those pages is a second copy of a World puzzle, and most of them are on Newspapers.com.

So we are rebuilding the series from its reprints. The record is the newyorkworld repository: one row per World Sunday, No. 1 to No. 211 (21 December 1913 to 30 December 1917), with every copy of each puzzle found in a partner paper, and a status page built from it.

The number in the grid

The World printed its series number inside the puzzle, in the shaded cross at the centre of its diamond grid. The Journal's first copy says No. 7; the next Sunday's says No. 8. One puzzle a week from No. 1 on 21 December 1913 fits exactly, which gives the record its one dating rule:

World No. n is expected on 21 December 1913 plus n−1 weeks.

Every World date in the record is an inference from that count, and the status page marks each one as such. What a page actually shows is recorded separately: the number printed in the grid, if any, and the paper's own date, which is a printing date, days to weeks after the World's. The Journal itself got the count wrong at first. Its heading on 1 February 1914 said "No. 5", with a line saying the fifth puzzle in the series was presented today, while the grid said No. 7; the next week the heading was gone. The grid number is the one that counts.

The Journal ran each puzzle on the World's own date through No. 21 on 10 May 1914. Then it fell behind, missing Sundays and running two to three weeks late, and skipped a number altogether: No. 33 on 23 August, No. 35 on 30 August, and No. 34 never. The last number printed anywhere is No. 54, in the Journal of 17 January 1915. After that no copy in any paper carries one, so from 1915 the count is all there is.

And the count may be wrong. The Journal's Thanksgiving puzzle is No. 46, printed on 22 November 1914; the count puts No. 46 on 1 November. Its Christmas puzzle, No. 50, falls on 29 November by the count. A paper would not run a Christmas puzzle a month early, so either the Journal was weeks behind by then, or the World itself skipped some Sundays in 1914 and the count runs early from there on. One dated World page from the second half of 1914 would settle it. Until then the printed numbers stay beside the inferred dates, and nothing is claimed as a World date that is not marked as inferred.

Printings are not puzzles

The tables keep the two apart. A Boston Globe Sunday in 1917 is a printing of a World puzzle, not a Globe puzzle. A printing with a number in the grid is tied to that World number. A printing without one is tied to another copy by its first clue and the shape of its grid, and the record says which copy and how. From the two dates, the paper's and the inferred World date, each printing's lag is computed, so the page shows how far behind each paper ran: the Globe on the World's own date in 1917, Pittsburgh one or two weeks later, Tacoma two, Los Angeles anywhere from three to eight.

Indexing a page means reading only what identifies it: the heading, the number, the credit line, the first clue, and which solution the page carries. Nobody types clues or answers into the tables. The puzzle text itself comes later, from the same OCR pipeline that read Judge, into an xd file with its proof beside it.

Chains: the order, proved page by page

Each Sunday's page also prints the solution to the previous week's puzzle. That is the second kind of evidence, and it does not depend on the count. A link says that the solution on page B answers the puzzle on page A: the same grid shape, and answers that fit A's numbering, checked by eye on the scans. The two pages need not be in the same paper. Tacoma's solution of 28 January 1917 proves the puzzle Los Angeles printed six weeks later.

A chain is a run of puzzles in which every adjacent pair is linked. The order of the puzzles in a chain rests on what was printed; only its place on the calendar rests on the count. Two chains are held in place by the printed grid numbers: Nos. 7 to 33, and Nos. 35 to 52. The long one is 107 puzzles from 21 November 1915 to 30 December 1917, 106 verified links, and it is dated by a solutions box the World Magazine printed with the World's own dates on it, which Tacoma reprinted on 22 July 1917. The Journal's pages from January to July 1915 form shorter chains with no number on any of them, so their order is proved but their World dates are not.

A break in a chain is a puzzle that no held page answers. Some breaks are the World's own missing weeks: the Journal never ran No. 34, and No. 53 is in no paper either. Some are a partner's: a page that is not on Newspapers.com is recorded as missing from the scans, never as a skip by the World. And some close with one page. The last break before the solutions box, between 11 and 18 February 1917, closed when a clipping of the Pittsburgh Press of 4 March 1917 turned out to carry the same puzzle as Shreveport's that day, which joined 107 puzzles into one dated run.

The chains have already corrected the count. The Journal printed one puzzle twice in the autumn of 1916, ran a week late for a month, then dropped a puzzle to catch up; nine World dates moved as a result. They also show where the method stops: from 1918 the Globe's solutions go on week after week, but its order stops being the World's. A puzzle another researcher dates to October 1919 ran in the Globe in May 1920, so after 1917 the Globe's pages are good copies with no World date, until dated copies from another paper plot the drift.

The Boston Globe's first crossword

The Globe printed its first crossword on 25 February 1917, on page 4, with no credit. The Tacoma Daily Ledger printed the same puzzle on 11 March 1917 as "Fun's Cross-Word Puzzle": the same grid, the same hand-lettered numbers 1 to 122, the same clues. So the Globe's first crossword was the World's, No. 167 by the count, and the Globe was one more FUN partner, running on the World's own date through 1917. The two copies differ only in typesetting: "clews" and "Mahometan" in Boston, "clues" and "Mohammedan" in Tacoma. The grid seems to have gone out as a ready-made plate or mat, and the clues as text that each paper set itself. That is an inference from two copies, but it fits every pair of copies we have compared since.

The Globe matters to the record for a second reason: Newspapers.com has its Sundays for 1917, and for fourteen of the World's 1917 puzzles the Globe is the only copy found so far.

Of the 325 Sundays looked for in a given paper through 1917, 184 turned up a page, 105 found nothing, 34 fell on a Sunday the paper carried no FUN puzzle, and 2 are missing from the scans. The quick "nothing found" results are not proof of absence: a text search misses pages whose type the OCR could not read, and some of those Sundays have not yet been browsed issue by issue.

The 56 World Sundays with no copy fall into a few groups:

  • Nos. 2, 3, 5 and 6, 28 December 1913 to 25 January 1914, before the Journal started carrying FUN. No. 1 has a copy because the Pittsburgh Press reprinted it in December 1918, almost five years later, and Nos. 2 and 4 are known from reproductions of the World's own pages in other researchers' work. Nos. 3, 5 and 6 are not in any partner paper on Newspapers.com; January 1914 has been searched thoroughly.
  • Nos. 34 and 53, 9 August and 20 December 1914, which the Journal skipped.
  • Nos. 55 to 100, 3 January to 14 November 1915: the Journal's pages from late January to July exist, about twenty puzzles in order, but with no number on them they cannot yet be tied to a World Sunday, and from August to mid-November the Journal ran a puzzle corner of its own instead. No other partner is known for 1915.
  • Nos. 146, 148, 179 and 184, four Sundays in 1916 and 1917 that are in no paper. One of them is settled: the World Magazine box goes straight from 17 June to 1 July 1917, so the World ran no puzzle on 24 June. The others are held open by the links on either side of them.

What would help most

The World's own FUN section for December 1913 and January 1914, Nos. 1 to 6. Four pages would close the gap at the start of the series, and they are the pages most likely to be in a private collection, on the New York Public Library's microfilm of the World, or in another researcher's files. A dated World page from the second half of 1914 would settle whether the count runs true. Any paper that carried FUN between August and November 1915 would date the Journal's floating chains. And a copy of any Sunday still open on the status page closes it in every paper at once.

If you have access to one of these, please open an issue with the date and where you found it, with a clipping link if there is one. Please don't upload page images: issues are public, and the pages come from Newspapers.com. The status page shows every Sunday, what is held for it and from where, the chains, and the next batch to look for.
