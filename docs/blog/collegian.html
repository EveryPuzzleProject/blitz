The start of a legendary career: Mike Shenk's Daily Collegian crosswords, 1976–81

Mike Shenk is one of the most prolific crossword constructors ever. His career began with a daily puzzle in Penn State's student paper, and we've indexed every one the university's open archive holds: 874 puzzles that no corpus has.

Every Puzzle Project · 6 November 2026

Mike Shenk has been making crosswords for nearly fifty years: for GAMES magazine, which he joined in 1981, and as the Wall Street Journal's crossword editor, among much else. It started at Penn State. According to the university's profile of him, he sent sample puzzles to the new editor of The Daily Collegian at the end of his freshman year and became the paper's first daily crossword constructor. Will Shortz is quoted there saying college-paper crosswords are usually weekly or less, and that Shenk is the only person he knows to have done one daily.

Those puzzles are the first five years of a major constructor's work, and none of them is in any crossword corpus. They exist only as page images in a university archive. Until this autumn, nobody had counted them.

How the index was built

The source is The Daily Collegian, 1940–2010 in the Pennsylvania Newspaper Archive, run by Penn State's libraries: open to anyone, with a high-resolution image and OCR text for every page. A program read the text of all 920 issues and scored each page for a crossword's signature: an "Across" and a "Down" heading, a run of numbered lines, a note on where the answers were. That found the puzzle on fewer than half the issues, because Shenk's byline was set as a graphic and the grid isn't text at all. For the rest, xword-ocr's grid detector looked at the page images, and found the main grid on 851 of the 874 puzzle pages.

Then a person settled every issue, on a page built for the job: all of an issue's pages as a contact sheet, with the program's guess marked. One click says which page the crossword is on, or that there is none that day. The 46 absent days got a second look, and 14 of them were checked again page by page. The gaps are mostly the thin summer issues of 1976 and 1977 and the last weeks of June 1981.

Beginning and end

The archive has no issues between 20 December 1975 and 10 March 1976. Its first issue after the gap, 11 March 1976, already has a crossword in the regular spot, unbylined; the next day's carries the first with Shenk's name. So his first puzzle is probably somewhere in those eleven missing weeks, and can't be pinned from this archive. If you have a Collegian from early 1976, or know of microfilm the digital archive missed, we'd like to hear.

The ending is clearer. The last crossword in the archive ran on 18 May 1981. From 10 June, the paper ran an ad in the puzzle's place: Mike Shenk had secured a job with GAMES magazine, and the Collegian was "looking for two or three bright, witty persons to take his place". It ran in at least nine issues, through 29 June. Penn State's profile says he made puzzles for the paper for three years; the archive says five, well past his 1979 graduation.

Two more oddities, found only because a person looked at every issue. On 20 January 1978 the front page reprinted the grid that had been left out of the previous day's paper, with an apology ("by mistake, honest!") and no clues, since the answers had already run. And a puzzle from 22 March 1976 is titled "Nazism", with black squares in the shape of a swastika and clues to match. The intent isn't recorded anywhere we've found; we'll keep the title as printed and flag the puzzle when it's imported.

What's next

An index is the first milestone. The goal is every one of these puzzles in xd, with its grid, clues, answers and a link to the page it came from, so they can sit in a research corpus beside the rest of Shenk's work. The remaining steps:

  1. Answer keys. The paper printed them elsewhere in the same issue until late 1977, in that day's classifieds until the summer of 1978, and in the next day's classifieds after that. A matcher finds each key by its black-square pattern; Shenk reused grids often enough (190 of 860 patterns appear more than once) that it also has to prefer the key nearest the puzzle's own issue. What it can't settle goes to a person.
  2. OCR into xd. The pipeline reads each puzzle from its page crop, checks it against the key, and writes the xd file with its proof.
  3. Review. Every reading goes on the review site beside its evidence, for people to check.

How you can help

  • Find the start. Daily Collegian issues from January to early March 1976, on paper or microfilm: tell us in the catalog.
  • Tell us about another college paper. Many ran a crossword, and many archives are open and searchable. A first-appearance date for any of them is a real contribution to the catalog.
  • Follow the Collegian's progress on its status page, and check puzzles against the scans once they reach the review site.
