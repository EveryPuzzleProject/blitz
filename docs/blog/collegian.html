Five years of a college daily's crossword, before its constructor was famous

From 1976 to 1981 Mike Shenk, later the Wall Street Journal's crossword editor, wrote a crossword for Penn State's student paper most days it printed. We've now indexed every issue in the university's open archive: 874 puzzles that no corpus has.

Every Puzzle Project · 6 November 2026

Mike Shenk is one of the best-known crossword constructors and editors of the last forty years: on the staff of GAMES magazine from 1981, and later the Wall Street Journal's crossword editor. Before any of that he was a mathematics student at Penn State, and the campus paper, The Daily Collegian, ran his crossword nearly every day it published. Penn State's own profile of him says he sent samples to the paper's new editor-in-chief at the end of his freshman year and became its first daily constructor. Will Shortz is quoted there saying that college-paper crosswords are usually weekly or less, and that Shenk is the only person he knows to have done one daily.

That run matters to the record for a simple reason: it's hundreds of early puzzles by a major constructor, and none of them is in any crossword corpus. Databases of published crosswords start with the big syndicated papers and magazines. A student daily in State College, Pennsylvania, isn't in any of them, however good its puzzle was. So the first five years of Shenk's work exist only as page images in a university archive, and until this autumn nobody had even counted them.

An open archive, with text

The source is The Daily Collegian, 1940–2010 in the Pennsylvania Newspaper Archive, run by Penn State's libraries. It's open to anyone, no login, and it's built the way Chronicling America is: every page has a high-resolution image served through IIIF, and every page has OCR text that the archive made itself. That second part is what makes a run like this tractable: a program can read the text of 920 issues in minutes and say which pages look like a crossword page.

So the harvest ran in that order. For each issue, a scan fetched the archive's OCR for each page, starting from the back of the paper, where the puzzle usually sat, and scored it for a crossword's signature: an "Across" and a "Down" heading, a run of numbered lines, a note saying where the answers were. A strong hit settled the page. A weak one, or none, went to a person.

There were a lot of weak ones. Text alone found the puzzle's page on fewer than half the issues. Shenk's byline was set as a graphic, not type, and the grid itself isn't text at all, so an issue whose clue columns the OCR had mangled looked, to the scan, like an issue with no puzzle. For those, the tools learned the paper's habits by era (the puzzle sat on page 4 for the first two years, then drifted deeper into the paper) and used that as a prior. Then xword-ocr's grid detector, the same one the project uses on Judge and GAMES pages, looked at each candidate page image for a crossword grid. It found the main grid on 851 of the 874 puzzle pages at first pass.

A person settles every issue

None of that is the final word. Every one of the 920 issues was settled by a person, on a review page built for the job: one issue at a time as a contact sheet of all its pages, with the scan's guess marked. One click says which page the crossword is on, or that there is no crossword that day. That click is recorded with who made it and when, and it always wins over the scan.

Saying "no puzzle today" is a stronger claim than "found it on page 4", so the absent days got a second look. Of the 46 issues without a puzzle, 14 were opened again and checked page by page. The gaps are mostly where you'd expect: the thin summer issues of 1976 and 1977, which came out two or three times a week, and the last weeks of June 1981.

The archive's page numbers are not the printed ones: they count scanned images in order, and from the autumn of 1979 most of those images are two-page spreads, so the archive's "page 7" can hold two printed pages. The index records the archive's numbering, since that's what the links need.

The oddities

A person looking at every issue finds things no scan would. Three are recorded in the index's exceptions file.

The apology. No crossword ran on 19 January 1978. The next day the front page carried the grid that had been left out, with an apology ("by mistake, honest!") and no clues at all: the answers had already appeared in the previous day's classifieds, so, the paper reasoned, readers couldn't possibly need anything else. The regular puzzle ran on page 4 of the same issue. We'll keep the reprinted grid as a stub, dated to the day it should have run, rather than lose it.

The help-wanted ad. The last crossword in the archive is dated 18 May 1981. From 10 June, in the space where the puzzle had been, the paper ran an ad instead: Mike Shenk, creator of the Daily Collegian crossword, had secured a job with GAMES magazine, and the paper was "looking for two or three bright, witty persons to take his place". It ran in at least nine issues, through 29 June. It's as clear an ending as a record could ask for, and it fits what the GAMES side of the project sees: Shenk's name is among that magazine's constructors in the run we're transcribing.

One flagged puzzle. A puzzle from 22 March 1976, in the run's second week, is titled "Nazism" and its black squares form a swastika, with clues to match. It's a deliberate theme and the intent isn't recorded anywhere we've found. We'll keep its title as printed and flag it when it's imported, so anyone meeting it in a corpus sees the note first.

Where it starts

The archive has no issues between 20 December 1975 and 10 March 1976. The first issue it does have, 11 March 1976, carries a crossword with no byline, and the next day's carries the first with Shenk's name. That's probably not his first: a puzzle already in its regular spot on the archive's first available day looks like a feature already running. The true start is somewhere in that eleven-week gap and can't be pinned from this archive, and puzzles from the missing issues aren't in the index, because we've never seen them. If you have a Collegian from early 1976, or know of microfilm the digital archive missed, we'd like to hear about it.

Penn State's profile says he made puzzles for the paper for three years. The archive says five, and past his 1979 graduation.

What's next

An index is the first milestone, not the last. The point is to get every one of these puzzles into xd, the plain-text format the project uses, each with its grid, clues, answers and a link to the page it came from, so they can sit in a research corpus beside the rest of Shenk's work and be searched and compared. That takes a few more steps, and the tools for each are built or under way:

  1. Page images. Each puzzle page is fetched whole, along with a native-resolution crop of the puzzle, into the folder layout xword-ocr reads.
  2. Answer keys. The Collegian printed the answers in three different places over the years: elsewhere in the same issue until late 1977, in that day's classifieds until the summer of 1978, and from then on in the next day's classifieds. A matcher finds each key by its black-square pattern, cut into small windows and compared against every puzzle's grid. Shenk reused grid patterns often enough, 190 of 860 patterns appear more than once, that the matcher also has to prefer the key in the puzzle's own issue or the one after. What it can't settle goes to a person, on a page that shows the candidate pages with the grids it found outlined.
  3. OCR into xd. The pipeline reads each puzzle's clues and grid from the crop, checks the answers against the key, and writes the xd file with its proof.
  4. Review. Every reading goes up on the review site beside its evidence, and people check what the OCR was unsure of.

One thing this run settled will shape how the next newspaper is done: a program for the cheap guesses, a person for the judgement, one click per issue. And the person who looks at every issue is the one who finds the apology, the ad and the themed puzzle. Those belong in the record too.

How you can help

  • Find the start. If you have access to Daily Collegian issues from January to early March 1976, on paper or microfilm, tell us in the catalog.
  • Tell us about another college paper. Shortz's remark suggests many campus papers ran a crossword, weekly or otherwise, and many of their archives are open and searchable. Our catalog has entries for about fifteen so far; a first-appearance date for any of them is a real contribution.
  • Follow the Collegian's progress on its status page, and the index itself in the repo.
  • Check puzzles against the scans once they reach the review site, by hand or by directing Claude through blitz.
